## 1. Phonology for a learner (pp. 8–33; context pp. 5, 36)

**Summary**
- The book uses two writing systems: the *local spelling* in quotation marks and a *phonemic transcription* in slashes. Stops (p, b, t, d, k, g) are never aspirated. t, d and n are **dental**, not alveolar as in English (pp. 8–10).
- The signature sound is the **voiced velar fricative**, spelled **"e"**. The book calls it a "fricative g" or "gargled g" (p. 14). It is unique among the West Visayan dialects, and one of the dialect's defining traits (p. 5).
- Where Hiligaynon and the other sister dialects have **l**, Aklanon usually has this *e*: *saeamat* for *salamat* ("thank you"), *eang* for *lang* ("just, only"). Proto-language *l* and *d* "reduced" to it (pp. 14–15).
- Aklanon **also has a real /l/**. It is found mostly next to *i*. It also appears when *e* meets the *-in-* infix and in metathesis, and in minimal pairs (p. 20). The "no l in Aklanon" claim is folk linguistics.
- The *e*-sound occurs mostly with *a* and *o*. It rarely occurs with *i*, and usually only through suffixation: *bakei* ("be bought for", from *bakae* + -i), *hambaei* ("be spoken to") (p. 15).
- The **glottal stop** is a full consonant. Spelling: unwritten at the start of a word and between two vowels; a hyphen between a vowel and a consonant; unwritten at the end of a word in traditional spelling (pp. 11, 32).
- There are three native vowels, **i, a, u**. The vowels **e** and **o** came in through Spanish and English but are now phonemic. Normally *o* and *u* alternate by position, as do *i* and *e* (pp. 24–28).
- Stress is phonemic. Default stress is on the **penultimate** syllable; the dictionary says that a word without an accent mark has penultimate stress (D. 38).

Allophones and loan sounds:
- [x] is how some speakers say *k* before *e*: *Akean* "Aklan", *mukeat* "open the eyes", *inakeanon* "speak Aklanon" (p. 10).
- [sh] is a variant of *s*. It is felt to be careless and is not accepted in prestige speech (p. 13).
- f is often reduced to p: *permi* "always" (Sp. *firme*), *presko*, *petsa* "date". v is usually b: *Bisaya'*, *bisita*, *biolin*, *bolkan*, *bapor*, *abokado*. v is kept only in some names (David, Victor) (pp. 12–13).
- ch and j can reduce to ts and dy: *litson*, *tsa*, *tsitsaron*; *dyanitor*, *dyoyn*. They never reduce in *cinelas*, *ceki*, *cans*, *caca*, *jet*, *jiyp*, *juwn*, *jaj* ("judge") (pp. 16–17).

- **Where *e* becomes l** (p. 20). It happens with the *-in-* infix: eaga' → linaga' ("boiled in water"); eahog → linahog ("acting naughty"); eumot → linumot ("covered with moss"). It also happens in metathesis: sueod → sudlan ("will be entered"); pueos → puslan; baeos → baslan ("will be revenged upon").
- **l next to i** (p. 20): libak ("gossip"), likod ("back"), alima ("hand"), ilo ("orphan"), uli' ("return; go home"), ulihi ("late, afterwards"), puril ("lie, deceive"), pensil.
- **Spanish loans that took the *e*-sound** (p. 15): *lagare* → eagari ("saw"); *corral* → kurae ("fence"); *casar* → kasae ("marry"). So the change is still productive.
- **Prestige** (p. 7): where two words exist, the one with *e* is felt to be "deep" or "pure". For "province", *eaeawigan* is preferred over *probinsya* or Tagalog *lalawigan*.

- **Three kinds of word-final vowel**, distinguished by what happens before a suffix (p. 12):
  - *Glottalized*: the consonant doubles (bata' → kabata-an).
  - *Followed by h*: the h surfaces (simba(h) → simbahan "church"; eskuyla(h) → eskuylahan "school"; tinda(h) → tindahan "market"; basa(h) → basahon "will be read").
  - *Open*: *a* is lost (pangasawa → pangasaw-on); *i* and *o* become semivowels (iti → ityan, tubo → katubwan).
- **Spelling** (p. 32): amo ("lord") has no mark; tao ("give"), huo ("yes"); man-o ("how much?"), ba-o ("turtle"), tam-is ("sweet"), sin-o ("who?").
- The dictionary marks a final glottal stop with **'** (baba', tubo') and an unwritten final h with **(h)**: simba(h), tinda(h), daea(h) (D. 3–4).

### 1.4 Vowels and the o/u, i/e alternations (pp. 24–28, 33; D. 35–36)

| Vowel | Notes and examples | Page |
|---|---|---|
| i | Tense in open syllables, lax in closed: ibah ("accompany"), itlog ("egg"), siki ("foot"), ihi' ("urine"), tubi' ("water"), puti' ("white") | 25 |
| e | Borrowed but phonemic: pare' ("compadre") / pari' ("priest"). Never starts a word (loans take i: *eskuyla* is said 'iskuylah). | o | Phonemic through loans: lolo ("grandfather") / Lulu; bos / bus; poro ("matches") / puro ("pure") | 27 |

**Where o and where u** (p. 28), with the spelling rules (p. 33):
1. In final syllables, especially open ones, the vowel is *o*; earlier syllables take *u*: limpyo, idto ("there"), aso ("smoke"), kamo; ulihi, sugid ("say, tell"), unga' ("child").
2. Otherwise, open syllables take *u* and closed ones take *o*: tsuper, upak, tulay ("bridge"); libon, libot ("go around"), ilong ("nose"), ka-on ("eat").
3. Unstressed vowels are *u*, except in a final syllable, where they are *o* (dahon "leaf", baon). Stressed vowels are *o* (sueod "enter", todas, sobra).
4. A stressed, closed final syllable can be *u* after b or m: namuk ("mosquito"), tabuk ("across"); but Ramon, sabon ("soap").
- **Spelling rule** (p. 33): in a two-syllable root, write *u* in the first syllable and *o* in the second: unga', sueod, uean ("rain"), bueong ("medicine"), humay ("cooked rice"), eupot ("paper bag"), huo ("yes"), gaeom ("[rain] clouds"). The dictionary extends this to longer roots, alternating back from the end: kutikot, abogado (D. 36).
- **Affixes keep a fixed spelling** whatever the root does: manogitlog, tagipuso-on, sumueunod (p. 33).
- **One-syllable words take o**: ro, do, ko, mo, eon, ron. Exception: the associate marker is spelled **ku**, to tell it apart from the pronoun *ko*: "baeay ko" ("my house") vs. "baeay ku amo" ("house of the master") (p. 33).
- **Reduplicated roots** repeat the same spelling: lolo, tonton, usus, bukbuk, kupkup, suksuk (p. 33).

### 1.5 Diphthongs, which the book treats as vowel + semivowel (p. 29)

Sequences (p. 29): ay (may "there is", baybay "beach", baeay "house"); aw (adlaw "sun, day", ikaw "you"); iw (isiw "baby chicken"); oy (baboy "pig", kahoy "tree"); uy (buyot "hold", uyon "like"); wa, ya, ye, yi, yo (wagi "left", yaya "nursemaid", tiya "aunt", yelo "ice", kalye "street", babayi "woman", pangadyi' "prayer", tiyo "uncle").

True diphthongs (p. 29): owa' ("none") [oua'], ayam ("dog") [aiam], iya ("here") [iia], uyon [oiun], tawo ("person") [tauo]. Spelling: always write *w* or *y*, never two vowels. Two vowels side by side are read with a glottal stop between them: huo, buot, siin, tao, pait (D. 35).

**Reading spelled "e"** (p. 33): next to a vowel, "e" is the consonant, as in baeay, kaeamay, daehon, eanggam, hueay. Next to a consonant or semivowel it is the vowel /e/, as in pangadye, kalye, presko, eskuyla, permi.

## 2. The three dimensions of Aklanon speech (pp. 5–7) and borrowed words (pp. 12–22, 133–134; D. 1–6, 25–31)

**Summary**
- The book describes the Aklanon of **Kalibo**, spoken by **middle-aged** people (p. 7). Kalibo speech is not "pure". It borrows from Tagalog and Ilongo and is full of colloquialisms. It is a good general model because it sits between conservative and heavily borrowing varieties (p. 5).
- Kalibo was chosen because it is the province's geographical, economic, educational and political center (p. 6). The book counts about 360,000 speakers (p. 7).
- There are **three dimensions** of variation: dialectal (town), age, and prestige.
- **Prestige Aklanon** is what religious use in sermons and politicians use in campaigns. It prefers *e*-words, longer structures, and **r over d** (*ro* not *do*). The *d*-forms are dismissed as baby talk ("inapa") or careless speech (p. 7).
- **Loans.** The dictionary deliberately includes "a very large representation of English and Spanish words". It says that eavesdropping on any conversation reveals "the overwhelming number of borrowed terms in actual use" (D. 1). **Neither book gives a percentage.** I counted tags in the OCR as a rough guide; these are my counts, not the book's, and OCR misses some tags: about 1,180 [Sp], 340 [Eng], 110 [Tag], 80+ [Hil] and 27 [Ch], against roughly 7,000 entry lines.
- **How loans are adapted.**
  - Sounds: f→p, v→b, ch→ts, j→dy; Spanish l/r → the *e*-sound; e and o are allowed.
  - Spelling: loans are respelled natively (bakwit, ayskrim).
  - Affixes: loans take native affixes like any root (pabulkit; RV5 *madyong*; *mamiso* "at one peso apiece").
  - Native equivalents: the dictionary gives the native word in brackets, e.g. **[Akl: dag-on]**.

## 3. Sentence patterns (pp. 50–51, 141–153, 163–164, 195–198)

**Summary**
- The **basic Aklanon sentence** has two parts, a **predicate** ("the news") and a **topic** (the concept uppermost in the speaker's mind). The normal order is **predicate first, topic second** (pp. 151, 197).
- The topic is marked **ro/do** for common nouns, **si** (singular) or **sanday** (plural) for personal names, or is a topic pronoun, topic deictic or function noun.
  - **ro and do are allomorphs** and generally interchangeable. *ro* is preferred, but *do* is preferred after /d, t, n/ (p. 143).
  - Prestige speech prefers *ro* (p. 7).
  - Town and country names take the **common** markers: *sa Kalibo*, *sa America*; *si* is only for people and pets (p. 143).
- Persons can **never be the direct object**. A person who receives an action is made the topic or put into the referent (*kay*, *kakon*) form. The book reads this as Philippine respect and indirectness (pp. 145–146).
- **Two topics** in one sentence give an identificational sentence. The first-stated topic is more important; the second is the news (p. 151).
- **hay** reverses topic and predicate (simple transformation). It also fronts any element for emphasis, roughly "well,…" (the "emphasis derivation") (p. 197).
- **may** gives existential, indefinite ("someone, something") and possessive statements (p. 198).
- Word order looks free but is not: *ro* + noun and *ku* + noun can swap places around the verb, but an associate phrase cannot open the sentence (pp. 50–51).

### 3.1 Function words by slot (pp. 146–147, 149)

| Class | Topic | Associate, before the noun (+ nga) | Associate, after the noun | Object | Referent |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Common noun | ro / do | ana ku … nga | ku | it | sa |
| Personal name | si / sanday | ay / anday | ni / nanday | — (uses referent) | kay / kanday |
| Pronoun | ako, ikaw, imaw, kami, kita, kamo, sanda | akon, imo … nga | nakon, nimo … | — (uses referent) | kakon, kimo … |
| Deictic | raya, ruyon, ranha', rato | kuraya … nga | kuraya, kuruyon, kuranhi', kurato | — (uses referent) | karaya / kara, karuyon / karon, karanha' / kana', karato / kato |
| Function noun | tanan, abo', sangkurot (unmarked) | anda tanan nga | ku tanan | it tanan | sa tanan |

Plural name markers are contractions (p. 149): sanday = si anda ay; nanday = ni anda ay; kanday = kay anda ay. Common nouns are pluralized with **mga**: ro mga isda'. *ro mga* and *do mga* contract to **rong** and **dong**.

### 3.3 Slot uses (pp. 142–146, 151–153)

| Point | Rule | Page |
|---|---|---|
| Markers make substantives | Whatever follows ro, ku, it or sa becomes a noun: ro gwapa ("the pretty one"); sa makapanaw ("to the one who can leave"); ku ikaarado ("with the one that can be used for plowing") | 149–150 |

| Wrong order | *Ku mga babayi ro isda' ginbaligya'* sounds foreign | 51 |

## 4. Counting, money, time, dates and age (pp. 36, 41, 126, 163, 166, 184, 190, 194, 201, 208; dictionary entries)

**Summary**
- The grammar has **no numeral chapter**. Pages 163–164 treat numbers only as members of the *function noun class* (abo', iba, sangkurot, tanan, isaea, daywa, tatlo, sambato). These stand unmarked as topics but take it, ku or sa in other slots.
- Most of this section therefore comes from **dictionary entries**. Numeral entries are spread out and some (apat, an-om) are badly OCR'd. **Native 11–99 forms are not given in either source** (see 4.1).
- The **enumerative ka** links a number or *pila* to the noun; *mga* is not used. If *ka* is dropped, the linker *nga* is obligatory; with *ka*, *nga* is optional (p. 190).
- Native numbers make **verbs** ("raise or lower to N, make into N") and **ordinals** with *ika-*. They make **distributives** with *tig-* ("N at a time").
- **Spanish numbers** are used for clock time and money: dos, tres, kwatro, singko, dyes, dose, kinse, beynte. The dictionary says *kwatro* is "used for counting time and money".
- *manga* (the same word as plural *mga*) means "about, approximately" with counts and clock times (p. 208).
