# Aklanon grammar reference from de la Cruz & Zorc (1968)

Source: de la Cruz, Basilio Sr. and R. David Zorc, 1968, *A Study of the Aklanon Dialect, Vol. 1: Grammar* (Kalibo, Aklan). ERIC ED145705, https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED145705.pdf (555,038 characters of OCR text).

## How to read these notes (important)

- Everything here came through the WebFetch tool, which returns a model-written summary of each ~100,000-character chunk, not raw text. Only 12 fetches were made (6 sequential chunks plus 6 targeted re-reads), and the summarizer repeatedly said it could not see some passages. Treat all forms as secondhand and verify against the PDF scan before putting anything into the app.
- The book's print-page numbers are quoted as the summarizer reported them. Many are marked `~` (approximate) because OCR page markers are garbled. Page numbers in the book's own table of contents (Unit Two p. 8, Unit Five p. 50, Unit Six p. 188, Appendix p. 212) are the most reliable anchors.
- **OCR uncertain** means the form looked garbled and the reconstruction is a guess by the summarizer. Where two spellings are shown (OCR vs. likely), the "likely" one is NOT confirmed.
- "Not in Zorc" means it was not found in the extracted text. It does not prove the book omits it, because several chunks were incomplete. Items I could not locate are listed in section 22.
- The book uses its own terminology: **function markers** (topic, associate, object, referent), **focus** (actor, object, instrument, referent), **associate pronouns** (roughly genitive), **referent** (roughly oblique/dative). It does not use "case" or "genitive". The book describes the Kalibo variety as the model dialect.
- The book distinguishes local spelling (in quotation marks) from phonemic transcription (in slashes) (Unit Two intro, p. 8).

## 1. Orthography and sounds (Unit Two, pp. 8-33)

### 1.1 The letter "e" (pp. 14, 32-33)

| Claim | Page |
|---|---|
| Aklanons spell the voiced velar fricative as "e" (the "ea" sound). Phonemic symbol is /ɣ/ in my reading; the book's transcription symbol is OCR-garbled throughout (appears as "dI", "Ai", "4"). | 14 |
| Spelling rule: "e" before a vowel is read as the consonant /ɣ/. "e" before a consonant or semivowel is read as the vowel /e/. | 32-33 |
| Tense mid-front /e/ occurs only in borrowings: /pare'/ "compadre" vs. /pari'/ "priest". | 25 |

Examples with "e" (p. 14): /ɣa'ay/ "dry, withered" (symbol garbled), /gabi/ "special; single out" (listed in the same group; OCR uncertain which entries belong to the /ɣ/ list).

OCR uncertain: several paired spelling/pronunciation examples on pp. 32-33 are corrupted (for example "/Y-ighmay/") and some pairs look mismatched.

### 1.2 Glottal stop (pp. 11-12, 26, 32)

| Claim | Page |
|---|---|
| The glottal stop is a phoneme. The book's symbol is rendered inconsistently by OCR ("1", "ft", "ri", "'"). | 11-12 |
| Minimal pairs: /bag'o/ "new" vs. /bago/ "before"; /bangko'/ "bench, seat" vs. /bangko/ "bank". | 26 |
| Spelling: initial glottal stops are not written; medial glottal stop between vowels is not written; between a vowel and a consonant (or a double glottal) a dash is written, for example "man-o", "sin-o", "tam-is". | 32 |

### 1.3 "o" versus "u" spelling (p. 33)

Source credited: the 1916 Laserna system, revised by Dr. Tommy Anderson (footnote 7). Four rules:
1. The o/u alternation applies to root words only, not to affixes.
2. One-syllable words use "o", except the associate marker "ku".
3. Identical reduplications are spelled by sound.
4. In two-syllable roots, first-syllable /o/ is spelled "u" and second-syllable /o/ is spelled "o".

OCR uncertain: several example words ("Lagipuso-on", "sumueunod").

### 1.4 Stress and pitch (pp. 30-31)

- Stress is phonemic. It distinguishes unrelated words, and it distinguishes noun from verb forms of related words: /hampángan/ "toy" vs. /hampangán/ "will be played with"; /pagkáon/ "food" vs. /pagkaón/ "while eating" (p. 30-31).
- OCR uncertain: accent marks were lost in most stress examples, so stress placement printed in the text cannot be trusted. Use the PDF scan.
- Pitch operates at sentence level (statement, question, exclamation, plus a pause mark) and changes intent, not word meaning. Illustrated with /iya/ "here" and /imaw/ "he/she" (p. 31). OCR uncertain: symbols for statement and exclamation both print as "/./", question symbol garbled, last gloss looks wrong.

### 1.5 Local alphabet (p. 32, §15)

The book lists native and borrowed letters and gives local pronunciations of foreign letters (for example "ñ" as /ny/, "ll" as /ly/, "x" as /ks/). OCR uncertain: several letter entries are corrupted, so the table is **not reproduced**; consult the scan.

## 9. The verb system (Unit Five B, pp. 55-120)

### 9.1 Terminology

The book analyzes a verb by **focus** (actor, instrument, object, referent), **mood** (simple, ability, accidental, imperative, etc.), **aspect** (real: imperfective, perfective, past conditioned; unreal: expected, future, subjunctive, dependent, future conditioned, participle) (p. 94), and **quality** (general, causative, distributive) (pp. 99-103). The book groups verbs into **classes I-IX** by the marker distribution (pp. 90-92) with sample roots: bakeo "buy", kaon "eat", abri "open", panaw "leave", bade "boil", baearilan "shoot one another", pamaroto "ride an outrigger".

## 21. Summary: what the book covers well and what it does not

1. Phonology and spelling (Unit Two, pp. 8-33) is thorough: glottal stop conventions, "e" = velar fricative rules, o/u spelling rules, stress as phonemic.
2. Pronouns are given as a systematic multi-set paradigm (topic, prepositive/postpositive/enclitic associate, referent) plus rich three-way deictic sets (pp. 157-163), though OCR damage limits what could be recovered.
3. Function markers are presented as a grid for common nouns, personal singular, and personal plural (pp. 146-148); it uses its own terminology (topic/associate/object/referent), not case names.
4. The verb system is the book's core (Unit Five B, pp. 55-120): focus, mood, aspect, quality, nine verb classes, and a master chart (p. 95). The chart is the single most valuable source and the most OCR-damaged one here.
5. Imperatives, ability, accidental, causative and distributive forms have usable examples with glosses.
6. Stative verbs (pp. 120-130), adjectives (pp. 169-178), and comparison/intensifier/degree machinery (ka-, pinaka-, mas, gid) are covered at length.
7. Discourse particles (pp. 199-209) are a strong section: about 20 particles with meaning, examples, and an ordering chart (p. 204).
8. Conjunctions, interrogatives and negatives are given as short lists with few examples.
9. Weak or absent: politeness/respect, frequency expressions, a numerals chapter, daw/gali/ngaron, kag/agod, and a stand-alone rule for the "hay" construction; there are no connected texts (only abbreviations and a bibliography after Unit Six).
10. This is a 1968 descriptive grammar using phonemic transcription, so modern spelling and forms must be checked with a speaker (and Kalibo is the model dialect).
